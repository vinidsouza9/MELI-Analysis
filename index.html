Introduction · 02

Key milestones, 1999–2026

From a Buenos Aires auction site to a regional commerce, fintech, and logistics ecosystem.

1999

Founded in Buenos Aires

Marcos Galperin and Hernán Kazah launch an eBay-style auction site for Latin America.

2001

eBay takes a strategic stake

Capital and credibility fuel early regional expansion beyond Argentina.

2003

Mercado Pago launches

Digital payments arrive for a region where most adults had no bank account.

2007

IPO on NASDAQ

MELI becomes the first major Latin American tech company on a U.S. exchange.

2026

Leadership transition

Ariel Szarfsztejn becomes CEO; the company now carries roughly a $95B market cap across 18 countries.

A 1999 auction site became a $95B commerce, fintech, and logistics ecosystem, profitable, and still growing faster than almost any peer in the region.

Introduction · 03

One platform, three businesses

Commerce funds fintech; fintech funds credit; logistics ties the ecosystem together.

I · Commerce

Marketplace

The original business: 84M unique buyers across 18 countries, fixed-price and auction listings, and Mercado Ads for sellers and brands.

II · Fintech

Mercado Pago

Serves 83M monthly active users with payments, credit cards, savings, and merchant lending; the fastest-growing, highest-margin segment.

III · Logistics

Mercado Envíos

Owned fulfillment centers and last-mile delivery built over two decades; a structural moat that's costly for rivals to replicate.

MELI is the only regional platform operating at scale across all three simultaneously.

Four engines, one loop, every turn makes the next turn stronger. Click a node.

Marketplace: Buyers and sellers meet; every transaction seeds the next engine.

Ghemawat's four threats and how the ecosystem answers each.

Imitation

Any single engine can be copied. Ten years of local logistics, payment trust, and transaction data cannot.

Hold-up

Own payments + own logistics = no dependence on banks or third-party carriers.

Slack

Data-driven operations, an "always paranoid" culture, continuous reinvestment in tech and infrastructure.

Substitution

Leaving means exiting commerce, payments, and credit at once; ecosystem-level switching costs.

Aligned · self-reinforcing · robust: a good business model by all three tests. How strong is it against Amazon and Shopee?

External Analysis · 04

Competitor landscape

Three fronts · ten factors · one uncomfortable finding.

Commerce

Marketplace scale, price, and brand trust; contested by Amazon, Shopee, AliExpress/Temu, and local incumbents.

Fintech

Payments and credit, MELI's most profitable flank; Nubank's only front of attack, and its most dangerous one.

Why MELI comes out ahead

Not by being best at one thing, but by being strong across all of them at once.

Integration beats point solutions

Commerce → payments → logistics → credit → ads reinforce each other and raise switching costs.

Logistics as a moat

Years of capital built fulfillment where infrastructure was poor; cross-border rivals can't cheaply match it.

Fintech data + trust

25 years of transaction data underwrite credit better than newcomers; buyer-protection trust is hard-won.

Local execution + profitability

Understands LatAm's fragmentation, tax, and currency chaos; and is profitable, so it outlasts subsidy-burners.

Sustained by capability, not by any single product

Applying Stalk, Evans & Shulman's “Competing on Capabilities” (HBR, 1992): advantage comes from cross-functional processes, not products or markets.

list→ship→pay→lend→repeat
Advantage lives in the process

MELI's edge isn't a product feature; it's the process running as one continuous loop.

The process creates visible value

Fast delivery, instant credit, and seamless checkout are outcomes customers feel directly.

Shared infrastructure, not silos

Logistics, fintech, and commerce share one data and infrastructure backbone.

Only the CEO can champion it

Cutting Brazil's free-shipping threshold sacrifices one unit's margin to strengthen the whole flywheel.

Why rivals can't just copy it: Nubank, Amazon, and Shopee each compete on a single product or function. Matching MELI means rebuilding an entire cross-functional capability, not shipping a feature. The real risk isn't a rival's product; it's MELI's own organization fragmenting back into silos, or regulation forcing the pieces apart.
